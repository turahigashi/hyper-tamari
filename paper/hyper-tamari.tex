\begin{abstract}
Let $\HH_r$ be the Goodstein hyperoperation hierarchy, so that $\HH_3$ is
exponentiation and $\HH_4$ tetration. For $r\ge3$ the operation is not associative, so
the value of $\HH_r(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ depends on the bracketing, that is on a binary tree
with $n$ leaves. Write $T\le_{\ev}U$ when $\ev_r(T)\le\ev_r(U)$ for \emph{every}
assignment of leaf values $\ge2$, and let $\le_{\St}$ be the Stanley order on those
trees, which extends the Tamari order.

At rank $3$ we determine $\le_{\ev}$ completely: it is the Stanley order. The inclusion
$\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$ is due to Chow; we prove its converse, which he left open,
constructively and with a bound. If $T\not\le_{\St}U$ then a \emph{single} leaf separates
them, and the value to put there is written down in advance as $M=2K^{2}$, where $K$ is a
product of subtree values read off from $U$. One family of $n-2$ assignments, given in
closed form, therefore decides the entire order.

For every $r\ge4$ we prove that $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$, the value increasing
strictly along every Stanley cover; the semi-associative law that underlies this is not
new, and we say where it is already stated. Equality in that law is settled by one
statement covering every rank and every boundary case. The device throughout is the
vector of right depths of the leaves. Much of the paper is machine-checked in Lean~4,
and it is explicit about what is not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The question}

Exponentiation is not associative, so the $C_{n-1}$ bracketings of $a_1\star\dots\star
a_n$ can take different values, and one may ask how those values are \emph{arranged}.
Two orders present themselves. The bracketings are the binary trees with $n$ leaves;
they carry the Tamari lattice, generated by right rotations, and, under the standard
bijection with Dyck paths, the Stanley order, in which one path lies weakly below
another. Against these stands the arithmetic order: write $T\le_{\ev}U$ when
$\ev_r(T)\le\ev_r(U)$ holds \emph{for every} assignment of values $\ge2$ to the leaves.

Spivak~\cite{MO} asked, for $r=3$, whether $\le_{\ev}$ and the Stanley order agree.
Chow, following a comment of Rubey, proved the inclusion $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$,
writing that he could ``complete half of the proof''; Rubey, independently, reduced that
inclusion to a single inequality and stopped there, writing ``it remains to check
that\ldots''. Chow decomposed the
whole problem into that inclusion together with its converse, and left the converse ---
that bracketings which are \emph{not} Stanley-related can be made to compare either way
by a suitable choice of leaf values --- open. Spivak's question already contains the
smallest instance of that converse: ``To see that $C$ and $D$ are incomparable: use
$a=b=c=2$ and $d=\text{large}$ to obtain $C>D$; and use $a=b=d=2$ and
$c=\text{large}$ to obtain $D>C$.'' What is missing there, and what Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} supplies, is how large
``large'' has to be, and that one leaf suffices at every $n$. The expected answer was not obvious: Stanley,
commenting on the question, pointed to a paper of Stembridge on a different order
attached to iterated exponentials and remarked that it ``lends credence to the
possibility that the iterated exponentiation order is not the Dyck order''~\cite{MO}.

Above rank $3$ the literature compares the bracketings of three arguments
(Section~\ref{sec:related}), which is enough to relate evaluation to the Tamari order;
the Stanley order, as far as we know, had not been related to evaluation there at all.
Theorem~\ref{thm:strank} below supplies one of the two inclusions, and the converse
remains open. The
behaviour of the relation with the Tamari order changes with the rank in a way that can
be pinned down exactly.

\subsection{Plan}

The paper has two parts. Part~I (Sections~\ref{sec:rank3}--\ref{sec:test}) settles
rank~$3$: the evaluation order \emph{is} the Stanley order, with an explicit separating
assignment and a finite test family. Part~II
(Sections~\ref{sec:rank4}--\ref{sec:stanleyrank}) goes up the hierarchy: the
semi-associative law at every rank, the complete classification of its equality cases,
the resulting dichotomy between rank $3$ and rank $\ge4$, and strict Stanley
monotonicity at every higher rank. Sections~\ref{sec:comp}
and~\ref{sec:formal} describe the computations and the Lean development,
Section~\ref{sec:related} the attribution, and Section~\ref{sec:open} what is left.

\subsection{Results}

Throughout, $\HH_r$ is the hyperoperation hierarchy fixed in
Section~\ref{sec:prelim}, with $\HH_1(a,b)=a+b$, $\HH_2(a,b)=ab$,
$\HH_3(a,b)=a^{b}$ and $\HH_4(a,b)=a\tw b$; $\Tam_n$ is the set of binary trees with
$n$ leaves, and $\ev_r$ their evaluation at leaf values $\ge2$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part I: rank three.} Here the answer is complete.

\begin{theorem}[Rank three: the evaluation order]\label{thm:main}
For every $n$ and all $T,U\in\Tam_n$,
\[
  T\le_{\ev}U\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T\le_{\St}U .
\]
\end{theorem}

The proof is constructive, and the witness it produces is unexpectedly simple: one leaf
does all the work, and the value put there is written down in advance.

\begin{theorem}[Separation by a single leaf]\label{thm:sep}
Let $T,U\in\Tam_n$ with $T\not\le_{\St}U$, and let $i$ be an index with
$r_i(T)>r_i(U)$ (Section~\ref{sec:coord} shows there is one). Put $K=K_i(U)$ as in
Definition~\ref{def:K} and $M=2K^2$. Then, for the assignment that gives leaf $i$ the
value $M$ and every other leaf the value $2$,
\[
  \ev_3(T)>\ev_3(U).
\]
\end{theorem}

Because the value $M$ can be bounded uniformly over all trees with $n$ leaves, a single
finite family of assignments decides the order.

\begin{corollary}[A finite test family]\label{cor:test}
Let $n\ge2$, let $F_0(t)=t$ and $F_{h+1}(t)=2^{F_h(t)}$, and put $M_n=2F_{n-2}(2)^2$. For
$3\le i\le n$ let $\vec a^{(i)}$ be the assignment with leaf $i$ equal to $M_n$ and every
other leaf equal to $2$. Then for all $T,U\in\Tam_n$,
\[
  T\le_{\St}U
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \ev_3(T)(\vec a^{(i)})\le\ev_3(U)(\vec a^{(i)})\ \text{ for } i=3,\dots,n .
\]
\end{corollary}

So an order defined by a universal quantifier over infinitely many assignments is
decided by $n-2$ of them, given in closed form. The first two leaves may be omitted
because $r_1(T)=0$ and $r_2(T)=1$ for \emph{every} $T\in\Tam_n$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:r12}), so those two coordinates never distinguish two trees.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Part II: up the hierarchy.} Above rank $3$ we do not know the analogue
of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}; what survives, and can be made exact, are the relations with
the Tamari and Stanley orders.

\begin{theorem}[Rank three: the local law]\label{thm:A}
Let $x\ge1$ and $y\ge2$. Then $(x^{y})^{z}\le x^{(y^{z})}$ for every $z\ge0$.
If moreover $x\ge2$, equality holds if and only if $z=1$ or $(y,z)=(2,2)$.
The hypothesis $y\ge2$ cannot be dropped: for $y=1$ and all $x,z\ge2$ the
inequality fails.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Rank $4$]\label{thm:B}
Let $x\ge2$ and $y\ge2$. Then $(x\tw y)\tw z\le x\tw(y\tw z)$ for every $z\ge0$.
If moreover $z\ge2$, the inequality is strict.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Tamari monotonicity]\label{prop:tamari}
Let $a\ge2$ and $r\ge3$. If $U$ arises from $T$ by a right rotation at some node (these
rotations are exactly the covers $T\lessdot U$ of the Tamari lattice), then
$\ev_{r,a}(T)\le\ev_{r,a}(U)$.
Consequently $\ev_{r,a}$ is order-preserving for the Tamari order. Moreover, for the
right comb $R_n$ with $n$ leaves,
$\ev_{r,a}(R_n)=\HH_{r+1}(a,n)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Strict Tamari monotonicity from rank four]\label{thm:strictcover}
Let $a\ge2$ and $r\ge4$. If $T\lessdot U$ is a Tamari cover, then
\[
  \ev_{r,a}(T)<\ev_{r,a}(U).
\]
Thus for $r\ge4$ the evaluation is strictly increasing along \emph{every} cover of the
Tamari lattice, not merely along root rotations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Rank dichotomy]\label{cor:C}
For $a\ge2$ the evaluation $\ev_{r,a}$ is order-preserving for the Tamari order at
every rank $r\ge3$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari}), and
\begin{itemize}
\item at rank~$3$ strictness can fail, but only for $a=2$: the cover
      $((aa)a)a\lessdot(aa)(aa)$ carries equal values, both $256$, when $a=2$;
\item at every rank $r\ge4$ it is strict on covers
      (Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover}).
\end{itemize}
In fact $(r,a)=(3,2)$ is the only exception: for every other pair with $r\ge3$ and
$a\ge2$ every cover is strict (Corollary~\ref{cor:coveq}).
\end{corollary}

\begin{theorem}[All ranks]\label{thm:D}
For every $r\ge3$ and all $x\ge2$, $y\ge2$ and $z\ge0$,
\[
  \HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,y),z\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r\bigl(x,\HH_r(y,z)\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Complete equality classification]\label{thm:E}
Let $x\ge2$ and $y\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For every $r\ge2$ the two sides are equal when $z=1$; in particular equality
      holds in Theorem~\ref{thm:D} for every $r\ge3$.
\item For $r=3$ the only further equality is $(y,z)=(2,2)$.
\item For $r\ge4$ and $z\ge2$ the inequality is \emph{strict}.
\item For every $r\ge3$ the inequality is strict when $z=0$, since
      $\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),0)=1<x=\HH_r(x,\HH_r(y,0))$.
\end{enumerate}
Items (1), (3) and (4) together give: for $r\ge4$ equality holds if and only if
$z=1$.
\end{theorem}

Theorems~\ref{thm:D} and~\ref{thm:E} are proved in Section~\ref{sec:general} by
inductions on the rank whose engine is the identity-like inequality \eqref{eq:SUM}
below; the case $r=3$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:A} and the case $r=4$ can also be
obtained directly, as in Section~\ref{sec:rank4}.

\begin{remark}[The statement is not an asymptotic triviality]\label{rem:nontriv}
Two features are worth isolating. First, \eqref{eq:HT} does not follow from
monotonicity alone: on the left the \emph{first} argument grows,
$x\mapsto\HH_r(x,y)>x$, while on the right the \emph{second} argument grows,
$z\mapsto\HH_r(y,z)$, so the two coordinates compete. Second, high rank does not
make everything large: since $\HH_r(a,1)=a$ for $r\ge2$, one gets
\[
  \HH_r(2,2)=\HH_{r-1}(2,\HH_r(2,1))=\HH_{r-1}(2,2)=\dots=\HH_2(2,2)=4
\]
for \emph{every} $r\ge2$. A degenerate corner therefore survives at every rank, and
it is exactly this corner that carries the rank-$3$ equality of
Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(2) and that has to be treated separately in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[How the two parts fit together]\label{rem:fit}
The Tamari order is contained in the Stanley order. At rank $3$, therefore,
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} contains Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} for $r=3$: Tamari
comparability implies Stanley comparability, hence comparability in $\le_{\ev}$. What
Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} adds is the ranks $r\ge4$, where Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has no
known analogue; and what Theorem~\ref{thm:E} adds at rank $3$ is the classification of
\emph{equality}, which an order statement does not see.
\end{remark}
\subsection{The mechanism, and what in it is not new}

Everything turns on one vector. Let $r_i(T)$ be the number of \emph{right} branches on
the path from the root to the $i$th leaf. The vector $r(T)=(r_1(T),\dots,r_n(T))$ does
two things at once:

\begin{itemize}
\item it presents the Stanley order coordinatewise: $T\le_{\St}U$ if and only if
      $r_i(T)\le r_i(U)$ for all $i$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:coord});
\item it counts the exponentiations through which an enlarged leaf value passes, and
      therefore the height of the tower that value generates
      (Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Both of these are already in the literature.} Knuth defines the Stanley lattice
(introduced by Stanley~\cite[pp.~222--223]{Sta})
on $n$-node forests by declaring $F\subseteq F'$ when the depth coordinates satisfy
$c_j\le c_j'$ for every $j$~\cite[\S7.2.1.6, exercise 28 and Fig.~41]{Knuth}; the passage
to binary trees is the identification of the depth of a vertex in a DFS tree with its
right depth, stated explicitly by Lehtonen and Waldhauser~\cite[\S2.5]{LW}, who in turn
attribute the underlying bijection to Cs\'ak\'any and Waldhauser~\cite[2.8]{CsW}
(``Bracketings are uniquely determined by their right (or left) depth sequences''); the
same material is restated in the Appendix of Waldhauser's dissertation~\cite{Wal}, whose
Proposition~A.7 repeats the argument of \cite[3.4]{CsW} for exponentiation.
And the second item is the key observation of~\cite[3.4]{CsW}: for exponentiation, ``the
height of the level of $p_i$ in $b$ always equals the right depth of $x_i$ in $B$''.
From this Cs\'ak\'any and Waldhauser deduce, by substituting distinct primes and
invoking unique factorisation, that distinct bracketings induce distinct operations. We
give a short proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:coord} in Section~\ref{sec:coord} only to
fix conventions and to connect the coordinates to the Dyck-path convention of~\cite{MO}.

The idea of enlarging a single variable is not new either: it is exactly what Chow
proposes in the last paragraph of his answer as the way to attack the missing half, and
Rubey, in comments under his own answer, expected it to work and sketched how the missing
half might be reduced to a simpler comparison, without completing the sketch.

What we supply is the step from \emph{difference} to \emph{direction}. Distinct primes
show that two values are unequal but say nothing about which is larger; to get the
order one needs a two-sided estimate for the tower that an enlarged leaf generates,
and that is Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}. With it in hand the remaining questions ---
which leaf to enlarge, how far, and why every other leaf may be left at $2$ --- all have
explicit answers.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{The hierarchy}

\begin{definition}[Hyperoperation~\cite{Good}]\label{def:hyper}
Define $\HH:\N\times\N\times\N\to\N$ by
\[
\HH_0(a,b)=b+1,\quad
\HH_1(a,0)=a,\quad
\HH_2(a,0)=0,\quad
\HH_{r+3}(a,0)=1,
\]
\[
\HH_{r+1}(a,b+1)=\HH_{r}\bigl(a,\HH_{r+1}(a,b)\bigr).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:conv}
For all $a,b$ we have $\HH_1(a,b)=a+b$, $\HH_2(a,b)=ab$ and $\HH_3(a,b)=a^{b}$.
Moreover $\HH_r(a,1)=a$ for $r\ge2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For ranks $1$, $2$ and $3$, induction on the second argument gives respectively
addition, repeated addition and repeated multiplication. The identity at second
argument $1$ is immediate at rank $2$. For $r\ge3$ the recursion gives
$\HH_r(a,1)=\HH_{r-1}(a,\HH_r(a,0))=\HH_{r-1}(a,1)=a$ by induction on $r$.
\end{proof}

The elementary growth and monotonicity consequences of this recursion are proved in
Appendix~\ref{app:elementary}; they are used in both parts.

We write $a\tw b:=\HH_4(a,b)$, so $a\tw 0=1$ and $a\tw(b+1)=a^{(a\tw b)}$.

\subsection{Bracketings and evaluation}

\begin{definition}[Trees and evaluation]\label{def:trees}
$\Tam_n$ is the set of binary trees with $n$ leaves; a tree is either a leaf or an
ordered pair $(L,R)$ of trees. Leaves are numbered $1,\dots,n$ from left to right.
Given $\vec a\in\N_{\ge2}^{\,n}$ we set $\ev_r(\text{leaf }i)=a_i$ and
$\ev_r\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=\HH_r\bigl(\ev_r(L),\ev_r(R)\bigr)$, and we write
$\ev_r(T)(\vec a)$ when the assignment is to be displayed. A tree together with an
assignment is a \emph{labelled tree}; rotation permutes no labels. When every $a_i$
equals a single value $a$ we write $\ev_{r,a}(T)$.
\end{definition}

At rank $3$ we abbreviate $\ev_3\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=\ev_3(L)^{\ev_3(R)}$, and we write
$T\le_{\ev}U$ when $\ev_3(T)(\vec a)\le\ev_3(U)(\vec a)$ for every
$\vec a\in\N_{\ge2}^{\,n}$; this is the \emph{evaluation order} of Part~I. In Part~II the
same symbol denotes the relation defined in the same way from $\ev_r$, at the rank $r$ under
consideration, which is always stated; above rank~$3$ it is a priori only a preorder, and
nothing below uses its antisymmetry. Every value
is at least $2$, since the leaves are and $\HH_r(x,y)\ge x\ge2$ for $x,y\ge2$ and
$r\ge2$; this is used without further comment.

\subsection{The two orders}

\begin{definition}[Right rotation and the Tamari order]\label{def:rot}
The \emph{right rotation at the root} sends $((A\cdot B)\cdot C)$ to
$(A\cdot(B\cdot C))$. The Tamari order on $\Tam_n$ is generated by right
rotations performed at arbitrary nodes; that this order is a lattice, with the left
comb as least and the right comb as greatest element, is the theorem of Friedman and
Tamari~\cite{FT}, whose title already names the relation a \emph{loi
demi-associative}; for its proof theory see Zeilberger~\cite{Zeil}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Dyck word and the Stanley order]\label{def:dyck}
Set $w(\text{leaf})=\varepsilon$ and $w\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=U\,w(R)\,D\,w(L)$. This is a
Dyck word of length $2(n-1)$. Its height function $H_{w(T)}(k)$ is the number of $U$'s
minus the number of $D$'s among the first $k$ letters. We write $T\le_{\St}U$ when
$H_{w(T)}(k)\le H_{w(U)}(k)$ for all $k$.
\end{definition}

With this convention the fully right-nested tree $(x_1,(x_2,(\dots)))$ has the highest
path and the fully left-nested tree the lowest, which is the orientation used
in~\cite{MO}. The Stanley order~\cite{Sta} \emph{extends} the Tamari order: $T\le_{\Tam}U$ implies
$T\le_{\St}U$, and the two differ already at $n=4$.

\begin{definition}[Right depth]\label{def:rdepth}
$r_i(T)$ is the number of right branches on the path from the root of $T$ to its $i$th
leaf; left branches are not counted. Thus $r(\text{leaf})=(0)$ and
\[
  r\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=\bigl(r(L),\,1+r(R)\bigr),
\]
where $1+r(R)$ means adding $1$ to each entry.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:r12}
For every $T\in\Tam_n$ we have $r_1(T)=0$, and if $n\ge2$ then $r_2(T)=1$. Consequently
two trees with the same number of leaves can differ in right depth only at an index
$i\ge3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $T$. A leaf gives $r=(0)$. For $T=(L,R)$ the first entry of $r(T)$ is the
first entry of $r(L)$, which is $0$ by induction. For the second entry there are two
cases: if $L$ is a leaf then $r(T)=(0,\,1+r(R))$ and the second entry is
$1+r_1(R)=1$; if $L$ has at least two leaves the second entry of $r(T)$ is $r_2(L)$,
which is $1$ by induction.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The semi-associative law, and one lemma used throughout}

The inequality
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:HT}
  \HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,y),z\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r\bigl(x,\HH_r(y,z)\bigr)
  \tag{HT$_r$}
\end{equation*}
is exactly the assertion that a right rotation, applied with $x,y,z$ the values of
the three subtrees, does not decrease the value.

The following single lemma is used in both parts of the paper: in Part~II it is what
makes \eqref{eq:HT} true at rank $3$ and, through the induction of
Section~\ref{sec:general}, at every rank; in Part~I it is one of the two absorption
steps of Section~\ref{sec:absorb}.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:mul-le-pow}
If $y\ge2$ then $yz\le y^{z}$ for every $z$. If moreover $z\ge3$ the inequality is strict.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The cases $z=0,1$ are immediate. For $z\ge1$ the induction step is
$y(z+1)\le2yz\le y\,y^{z}=y^{z+1}$, using $z+1\le2z$ and, for the middle inequality,
$2z\le yz\le y^{z}$. For strictness, $3y<y^{3}$ because $y^{2}\ge4>3$; and if
$yz<y^{z}$ with $z\ge3$, then $y(z+1)\le2yz<2y^{z}\le y^{z+1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:mul-eq-pow}
If $y\ge2$ then $yz=y^{z}$ if and only if $z=1$ or $(y,z)=(2,2)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $z=0$ then $yz=0\ne1=y^{z}$. If $z=1$ both sides are $y$. If $z=2$ then
$yz=y^{z}$ reads $2y=y^{2}$, i.e.\ $y=2$ after cancelling $y>0$. If $z\ge3$ the
inequality is strict by Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow}. The converse is immediate.
\end{proof}

\part*{Part I. Rank three: the evaluation order}
\section{Rank three: the semi-associative law}\label{sec:rank3}

At rank $3$ the collapsing law $(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$ reduces \eqref{eq:HT} to a
comparison of exponents.


\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}]
Since $(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$, the inequality follows from
Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow} and monotonicity of $t\mapsto x^{t}$ for $x\ge1$.
For $x\ge2$ the map $t\mapsto x^{t}$ is injective, so equality holds iff
$yz=y^{z}$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:mul-eq-pow} applies. For the last claim,
$(x^{1})^{z}=x^{z}$ while $x^{(1^{z})}=x$, and $x^{z}>x$ whenever $x,z\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Failure of strictness]\label{ex:collide}
Take $a=2$ and $n=4$. The five bracketings evaluate under $\HH_3$ to
\[
  ((aa)a)a=(a(aa))a=(aa)(aa)=256,
  \qquad
  a((aa)a)=a(a(aa))=65536 .
\]
In particular the Tamari cover $((aa)a)a\lessdot(aa)(aa)$ has equal endpoints,
so $\ev_{3,2}$ is monotone but not strictly monotone; it is also not injective.
\end{example}

The coincidence $(a(aa))a=(aa)(aa)$ is not special to $a=2$: for every $a$ both sides
equal $a^{a^{a+1}}$. G\"obel and Nederpelt~\cite{GN} already use this pair to show that
different bracketings can have the same value when every leaf carries the same real
number $x>1$. Their standard form turns a bracketing with $n$ leaves into a rooted tree
with $n-1$ edges, and bracketings with the same tree have the same value; hence
$\ev_{3,a}$ takes at most as many values on $\Tam_n$ as there are rooted trees with
$n-1$ edges~\cite[Theorem~2.2]{GN}, fewer than $C_{n-1}$ once $n\ge4$. Guy and
Selfridge~\cite{GS} note the same identity in their study of how many distinct values
the bracketings of $a\star a\star\dots\star a$ take under exponentiation, and conclude
that ``exponentiation is not completely non-associative''.
\section{The Stanley order in right-depth coordinates}\label{sec:coord}

\begin{proposition}[Coordinate presentation; known]\label{prop:coord}
For $T,U\in\Tam_n$,
\[
  T\le_{\St}U
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  r_i(T)\le r_i(U)\ \text{ for all } i=1,\dots,n .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $q(\text{leaf})=\varepsilon$ and $q\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=q(L)\,U\,q(R)\,D$; this is the
word obtained from postfix notation by writing $U$ for a leaf and $D$ for an operator
and deleting the initial $U$. An induction on the tree shows that $q(T)$ is a Dyck word
and that the height immediately after its $j$th up-step equals $r_{j+1}(T)$: in
$q(L)\,U\,q(R)\,D$ the up-steps of $q(L)$ keep their heights, the displayed $U$ has
height $1$, and the up-steps of $q(R)$ have their heights raised by $1$, which matches
Definition~\ref{def:rdepth}.

Let $p_j(T)$ be the position of the $j$th up-step, positions counted from $1$. Among the
first $p_j$ letters there are $j$ up-steps and $p_j-j$ down-steps, so
$r_{j+1}(T)=2j-p_j(T)$. Since the height at position $k$ is $2u(k)-k$ with $u(k)$ the
number of up-steps so far, one Dyck word lies weakly below another exactly when each of
its up-steps occurs no earlier, i.e.\ exactly when $p_j$ is weakly larger for every $j$,
i.e.\ exactly when $r_{j+1}$ is weakly smaller for every $j$.

Finally, write $\bar u$ for the word obtained from $u$ by reversing it and exchanging
$U$ with $D$. Then $\overline{q(T)}=w(T)$: indeed
$\overline{q(L)\,U\,q(R)\,D}=U\,\overline{q(R)}\,D\,\overline{q(L)}$, which is
$U\,w(R)\,D\,w(L)=w\bigl((L,R)\bigr)$ by induction. For a word $u$ of length $N$ with
equally many $U$'s and $D$'s one has $H_{\bar u}(k)=H_u(N-k)$, since the first $k$
letters of $\bar u$ are the last $k$ letters of $u$ with the two symbols exchanged.
Hence $q(T)$ lies weakly below $q(U)$ if and only if $w(T)$ does below $w(U)$, and the
comparison may be read off in either convention.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:coordknown}
As said in the introduction, Proposition~\ref{prop:coord} is known. Knuth's exercise 28
defines the Stanley lattice on $n$-node forests by comparing depth coordinates, and his
Fig.~41 lists the $14$ forests of order $4$ by those coordinates~\cite{Knuth}. The
passage between his indexing and ours is the shift $c_j=r_{j+1}-1$, which comes from
deleting the root: a tree with $n$ leaves gives a forest with $n-1$ nodes. Under it the
five vectors of Example~\ref{ex:four} become the order-$3$ sequences $012$, $011$,
$001$, $010$, $000$, and the fourteen vectors for $n=5$ reproduce, as a set, exactly the
fourteen sequences printed in Fig.~41. That right-depth sequences determine the bracketing is
$2.8$ of~\cite{CsW}, where the sequences occurring are also characterised (in their
notation, with $d_i=r_i$, they are the $(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ with $d_1=0$, $d_2=1$ and
$1\le d_{i+1}\le d_i+1$); the identification
with DFS depth is~\cite[\S2.5]{LW}.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}\label{ex:four}
For $n=4$ the five trees have right-depth vectors
\[
  \begin{array}{lll}
  (a(b(cd)))&(0,1,2,3)\qquad& ((a(bc))d)\ \ (0,1,2,1)\\
  (a((bc)d))&(0,1,2,2)& (((ab)c)d)\ \ (0,1,1,1)\\
  ((ab)(cd))&(0,1,1,2)&
  \end{array}
\]
Exactly one pair is incomparable, namely $((ab)(cd))$ and $((a(bc))d)$: their
vectors $(0,1,1,2)$ and $(0,1,2,1)$ differ in both directions. This is the pair
called $C$ and $D$ in~\cite{MO}.
\end{example}
\subsection{The covering relation}

The next two lemmas describe the covers of the Stanley order. They are used twice: in
Section~\ref{sec:chow} for rank three and in Section~\ref{sec:stanleyrank} for every
rank above it.

\begin{lemma}[Cover description]\label{lem:stcover}
$T\lessdot U$ in the Stanley order if and only if $w(U)$ is obtained from $w(T)$ by
replacing one occurrence of $DU$ by $UD$.
\end{lemma}

This is the answer to Knuth's exercise 28(c)~\cite{Knuth}, where the same operation is
described on nested parenthesis strings as ``changing \texttt{)(} to \texttt{()}''; in
the discussion of~\cite{MO} it is also stated, in terms of peaks and valleys by Rubey
and in reverse Polish form by Chow. We use it as known and claim no
priority for it. For completeness, and so that the reader need not consult the artifact,
here is a direct proof; a machine-checked proof is also included
(Section~\ref{sec:formal}), because the formalisation of Section~\ref{sec:stanleyrank}
rests on it.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:stcover}]
Replacing an occurrence of $DU$ by $UD$ raises exactly one height, by $2$, and leaves
the word balanced and nonnegative. No word lies strictly between the two: they agree at
every other position, and at the position in question a height must have the parity of
its index, so a value strictly between $h-1$ and $h+1$ is impossible.

Conversely let $w<v$ be Dyck words of the same length, and let $k$ be the first position
at which they differ. The heights agree before $k$, so $w$ has $D$ and $v$ has $U$
there, and at position $k$ the height of $v$ exceeds that of $w$ by exactly $2$. Let
$\ell>k$ be the first position after $k$ at which $w$ has $U$; such a position exists,
because the two words have equally many up-steps and $v$ has used one more of them by
position $k$. Between $k$ and $\ell-1$ the word $w$ takes only down-steps, while each
letter of $v$ changes its height by $\pm1$; so on that range the excess of the height of
$v$ over that of $w$ never decreases, and it is at least $2$ throughout. Swapping the
$DU$ that
$w$ carries at positions $\ell-1,\ell$ therefore produces a Dyck word $w'$ with
$w<w'\le v$, and if $v$ covers $w$ then $w'=v$. The first-return decomposition
identifies Dyck words with trees, so the statement transfers to the Stanley order on
trees.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Local form of a cover]\label{lem:localcover}
Every tree $R$ factors uniquely along its leftmost leaf as
\[
  R=L_{\mathbf D}(c):=\bigl(\cdots((c\cdot D_1)\cdot D_2)\cdots D_m\bigr),
  \qquad m\ge0,
\]
where $c$ is that leaf. A pair $T\lessdot U$ of the Stanley order is exactly a pair
\[
  T=\bigl((A\cdot B)\cdot L_{\mathbf D}(c)\bigr)
  \ \lessdot\
  U=\bigl(A\cdot L_{\mathbf D}\bigl((B\cdot c)\bigr)\bigr)
\]
occurring inside a common tree context.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The factorisation is obtained by peeling off left children until the leftmost leaf is
reached, and is clearly unique. Write
\[
  V=U\,w(D_m)\,D\cdots U\,w(D_1)\,D .
\]
Since $w$ of a leaf is empty, the recursion $w\bigl((L,R)\bigr)=U\,w(R)\,D\,w(L)$
gives $w\bigl(L_{\mathbf D}(c)\bigr)=V$, and hence
\[
  w(T)=U\,V\,\mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{U}\,w(B)\,D\,w(A),
  \quad
  w(U)=U\,V\,\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{D}\,w(B)\,D\,w(A),
\]
the second because $w\bigl(L_{\mathbf D}((B\cdot c))\bigr)=V\,U\,D\,w(B)$. So the
displayed pair replaces exactly one $DU$ by $UD$.

Conversely, let a $DU$ be given in $w(T)=U\,w(R)\,D\,w(L)$. It lies inside $w(R)$,
inside $w(L)$, or consists of the $D$ following $w(R)$ together with the first letter of
$w(L)$; the last happens exactly when $w(L)$ is non-empty, that is when $L=(A\cdot B)$,
and then $T$ has the displayed form with $R=L_{\mathbf D}(c)$. The first two cases are
the same statement for a subtree, so induction on $T$ finishes the proof.
\end{proof}


\section{Absorbing a power into a tower}\label{sec:absorb}

\begin{definition}\label{def:F}
$F_0(t)=t$ and $F_{h+1}(t)=2^{F_h(t)}$. All arguments below are integers $\ge2$, and
each $F_h$ is strictly increasing on them. Two facts are used repeatedly and follow by
induction on $h$: $F_h(t)\le F_{h+1}(t)$, because $z\le2^{z}$; and
\[
  F_{h+1}(t)=F_h\bigl(2^{t}\bigr),
\]
so that replacing the argument $t$ by $2^{t}$ and adding one to the height are the same
operation.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:basic}
For every integer $c\ge1$ we have $c\le2^{c-1}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $c$: it holds at $c=1$, and $c+1\le 2c\le2\cdot2^{c-1}$.
\end{proof}

Together with Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow}, which gives $cy\le y^{c}$ for $c\ge1$ and
$y\ge2$, this is all the elementary arithmetic the present section needs. Note that
Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow} is the same inequality that drives the whole hierarchy in
Part~II; it is used here in the form $c\,y\le y^{c}$ and there in the form
$yz\le y^{z}$.

\begin{lemma}[Absorption]\label{lem:absorb}
For all $h\ge0$, integers $z\ge2$ and $c\ge1$,
\[
  F_h(z)^{\,c}\ \le\ F_h(z^{c}).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $h$. At $h=0$ both sides equal $z^{c}$. Assuming the claim at $h$,
\[
  F_{h+1}(z)^{c}=2^{\,c\,F_h(z)}\le2^{\,F_h(z)^{c}}\le2^{\,F_h(z^{c})}=F_{h+1}(z^{c}),
\]
the first inequality by Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow} with $y=F_h(z)\ge2$ and the second by the
induction hypothesis.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Absorbing the base]\label{lem:base}
For integers $c,y\ge2$ we have $c^{\,y}\le2^{\,y^{c}}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$c^{y}\le(2^{c})^{y}=2^{cy}\le2^{y^{c}}$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow}.
\end{proof}
\section{Bounds when a single leaf is enlarged}\label{sec:bounds}

\begin{definition}\label{def:K}
For a tree $T$ and a leaf index $i$, walk from that leaf to the root. At each step the
path leaves a sibling subtree behind; let $c_1,\dots,c_m$ be the values of those sibling
subtrees when \emph{all} their leaves are set to $2$, and put
\[
  K_i(T)=\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}c_\nu ,
\]
the empty product being $1$. Each $c_\nu$ is an integer $\ge2$ and does not depend on the
value placed at leaf $i$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:vTi}
$v_{T,i}(t)=\ev_3(T)(2,\dots,2,t,2,\dots,2)$, the value of $T$ when leaf $i$ carries $t$
and every other leaf carries $2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Bounds]\label{prop:bounds}
For every integer $t\ge2$,
\[
  F_{r_i(T)}(t)\ \le\ v_{T,i}(t)\ \le\ F_{r_i(T)}\bigl(t^{K_i(T)}\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Induction along the path from leaf $i$ to the root. At the leaf the value is $t$, the
right depth is $0$ and the product is $1$, so both bounds are equalities.

Suppose the current subtree has value $v$, right depth $h$ so far and sibling product
$K$ so far, and that $F_h(t)\le v\le F_h(t^{K})$. Let $c\ge2$ be the value of the next
sibling subtree, all of its leaves being $2$.

If the subtree containing leaf $i$ is the \emph{left} child, the new value is $v^{c}$,
the right depth is unchanged and the product becomes $Kc$. Then
$v^{c}\ge v\ge F_h(t)$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:absorb},
\[
  v^{c}\le F_h(t^{K})^{c}\le F_h\bigl((t^{K})^{c}\bigr)=F_h(t^{Kc}).
\]

If it is the \emph{right} child, the new value is $c^{\,v}$, the right depth becomes
$h+1$ and the product becomes $Kc$. Then $c^{v}\ge2^{v}\ge2^{F_h(t)}=F_{h+1}(t)$, and by
Lemmas~\ref{lem:base} and~\ref{lem:absorb},
\[
  c^{v}\le2^{\,v^{c}}\le2^{\,F_h(t^{K})^{c}}\le2^{\,F_h(t^{Kc})}=F_{h+1}(t^{Kc}).
\]
Either way the hypothesis is restored, and at the root it is the assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:csw}
The qualitative content of Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds} --- that $r_i(T)$ is the level
at which leaf $i$ sits in the tower --- is the observation of $3.4$ in~\cite{CsW}. What
the proposition adds is the two-sided estimate, and in particular the upper bound, in
which the whole effect of the other leaves is compressed into the single factor
$K_i(T)$ in the exponent of the argument.
\end{remark}
\section{The Stanley order at rank three}\label{sec:chow}

The inclusion $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$ at rank three is Chow's, proved
in~\cite{MO} by an argument on reverse Polish notation. With
Lemmas~\ref{lem:stcover} and~\ref{lem:localcover} in hand it has a short direct proof,
which we give because it makes Theorem~\ref{thm:main} self-contained and because the
same computation locates the equality cases. \textbf{The priority is Chow's}; what
follows is an alternative proof, not a new result.

\begin{theorem}[Stanley monotonicity at rank three]\label{thm:chow}
Let $T,U\in\Tam_n$ carry the same leaf labels, all $\ge2$. If $T\le_{\St}U$ then
$\ev_3(T)\le\ev_3(U)$. Consequently $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:stcover} it suffices to treat one cover, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:localcover} we may take
\[
  T=\bigl((A\cdot B)\cdot L_{\mathbf D}(c)\bigr),
  \qquad
  U=\bigl(A\cdot L_{\mathbf D}\bigl((B\cdot c)\bigr)\bigr)
\]
inside a common context. Write $a,b$ for the values of $A,B$, let $c$ also denote the
value of the leftmost leaf of $L_{\mathbf D}(c)$, and let $d_1,\dots,d_m$ be the values
of $D_1,\dots,D_m$; all are $\ge2$. At rank three the collapsing law
$(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$ gives
\[
  G(t):=\bigl(\cdots((t^{d_1})^{d_2})\cdots\bigr)^{d_m}=t^{K},
  \qquad K:=d_1d_2\cdots d_m\ \ge1,
\]
the empty product being $1$. Hence
\[
  \ev_3(T)=(a^{b})^{G(c)}=a^{\,b\,c^{K}},
  \qquad
  \ev_3(U)=a^{\,G(b^{c})}=a^{\,(b^{c})^{K}}=a^{\,b^{Kc}} ,
\]
and since $a\ge2$ it is enough to compare the exponents. By Lemma~\ref{lem:basic},
$c\le2^{c-1}\le b^{c-1}$, so $c^{K}\le b^{K(c-1)}$ and therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chow}
  b\,c^{K}\ \le\ b^{\,K(c-1)+1}\ \le\ b^{\,Kc},
\end{equation}
the last step because $K\ge1$. So $\ev_3(T)\le\ev_3(U)$, and since $\HH_3$ is
increasing in each argument on values $\ge2$ the inequality propagates through the
surrounding context. Chaining covers gives the theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Equality along a Stanley cover at rank three]\label{cor:chowcase}
In the situation above, $\ev_3(T)=\ev_3(U)$ if and only if $m=0$ and $b=c=2$; that is,
if and only if $L_{\mathbf D}(c)$ is a leaf and both it and $B$ are leaves labelled $2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The second inequality of \eqref{eq:chow} is strict unless $K(c-1)+1=Kc$, that is unless
$K=1$; and $K=1$ forces $m=0$, since every $d_j$ is at least $2$. When $K=1$ the first
inequality reads $bc\le b^{c}$, which by Proposition~\ref{prop:mul-eq-pow} is an equality
exactly when $c=1$ or $(b,c)=(2,2)$; the first is excluded because $c\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:m0tamari}
When $m=0$ the cover of Lemma~\ref{lem:localcover} reads
$((A\cdot B)\cdot c)\to(A\cdot(B\cdot c))$, which is a Tamari rotation. So
Corollary~\ref{cor:chowcase} contains Corollary~\ref{cor:coveq} at rank three, and says
in addition that a Stanley cover which is \emph{not} a Tamari rotation --- one with
$m\ge1$ --- is always strict.
\end{remark}

\section{Separation, and the main theorem}\label{sec:sep}

\begin{lemma}[Threshold]\label{lem:threshold}
For every integer $K\ge1$, putting $M=2K^{2}$ we have $2^{M}>M^{K}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:basic}, $K\le2^{K-1}$, so
$M^{K}=(2K^{2})^{K}\le\bigl(2\cdot2^{2(K-1)}\bigr)^{K}=2^{(2K-1)K}<2^{2K^{2}}=2^{M}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sep}]
Write $s=r_i(U)$, $K=K_i(U)$ and $M=2K^{2}$, and give leaf $i$ the value $M$ with every
other leaf equal to $2$. By hypothesis $r_i(T)\ge s+1$. Using
Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds} on both sides, the monotonicity of the $F_h$ in $h$ for
arguments $\ge2$, the identity $F_{s+1}(M)=F_s(2^{M})$, the strict monotonicity of $F_s$
and Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold},
\[
  v_{T,i}(M)\ \ge\ F_{r_i(T)}(M)\ \ge\ F_{s+1}(M)\ =\ F_s(2^{M})
  \ >\ F_s(M^{K})\ \ge\ v_{U,i}(M). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
The implication $T\le_{\St}U\Rightarrow T\le_{\ev}U$ is
Theorem~\ref{thm:chow}. For the converse, suppose $T\not\le_{\St}U$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:coord} there is an index $i$ with $r_i(T)>r_i(U)$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} produces an assignment at which $\ev_3(T)>\ev_3(U)$; hence
$T\not\le_{\ev}U$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:distinct}
For every $n$ the $C_{n-1}$ trees in $\Tam_n$ induce pairwise distinct functions on
$\N_{\ge2}^{\,n}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $T\ne U$ then $r(T)\ne r(U)$, by Proposition~\ref{prop:coord} and the antisymmetry
of $\le_{\St}$ (or directly by $2.8$ of~\cite{CsW}), so some coordinate differs and
Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} separates the two values.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:spectrum}
Corollary~\ref{cor:distinct} says that the associative spectrum of exponentiation on
$\N_{\ge2}$ is the Catalan number $C_{n-1}$, which is $3.4$ of~\cite{CsW}; that proof
substitutes distinct primes and appeals to unique factorisation. The present argument
recovers it and says in addition which of the two values is the larger.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:bothways}
Applying Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} to the ordered pairs $(T,U)$ and $(U,T)$ in turn shows
that two Stanley-incomparable trees are separated in \emph{both} directions, by two
assignments each of which differs from $(2,\dots,2)$ in a single coordinate. This is the
statement Chow left open.
\end{remark}
\section{A uniform finite test family}\label{sec:test}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:prod}
For all $p,q\ge0$,
\[
  \text{(i) } F_p(2)\,F_q(2)\le F_{p+q+1}(2),
  \qquad
  \text{(ii) } F_p(2)^{\,F_q(2)}\le F_{p+q+1}(2).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every $F_h(2)$ is one of $2,4,16,65536,\dots$, and $z^{2}\le2^{z}$ holds at $z=2$, at
$z=4$ and for every real $z\ge4$; hence $F_h(2)^{2}\le2^{F_h(2)}=F_{h+1}(2)$ for all
$h$. For (i) we may assume $p\le q$, and then
$F_p(2)F_q(2)\le F_q(2)^{2}\le F_{q+1}(2)\le F_{p+q+1}(2)$. For (ii), if $p=0$ then
$F_0(2)^{F_q(2)}=2^{F_q(2)}=F_{q+1}(2)$; and if $p\ge1$ then $F_p(2)=2^{F_{p-1}(2)}$, so
by (i)
\[
  F_p(2)^{\,F_q(2)}=2^{\,F_{p-1}(2)F_q(2)}\le2^{\,F_{p+q}(2)}=F_{p+q+1}(2). \qedhere
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:maxval}
A tree with $k$ leaves has value at most $F_{k-1}(2)$ when every leaf carries $2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on the tree. A leaf gives $2=F_0(2)$. A tree $(L,R)$ with $k_L$ and $k_R$
leaves gives $\ev_3(L)^{\ev_3(R)}\le F_{k_L-1}(2)^{\,F_{k_R-1}(2)}\le F_{k_L+k_R-1}(2)$, the
last step by Lemma~\ref{lem:prod}(ii).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:Kbound}
For every $n\ge2$, every $T\in\Tam_n$ and every leaf $i$, $K_i(T)\le F_{n-2}(2)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The sibling subtrees met on the path from leaf $i$ to the root partition the remaining
$n-1$ leaves; let $k_1,\dots,k_m$ be their numbers of leaves, so $\sum_\nu k_\nu=n-1$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:maxval}, $c_\nu\le F_{k_\nu-1}(2)$. Applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:prod}(i) $m-1$ times,
\[
  K_i(T)=\prod_{\nu=1}^{m}c_\nu\ \le\ \prod_{\nu=1}^{m}F_{k_\nu-1}(2)
  \ \le\ F_{\left(\sum_\nu k_\nu\right)-1}(2)=F_{n-2}(2),
\]
since $F_{a-1}(2)F_{b-1}(2)\le F_{a+b-1}(2)$ is Lemma~\ref{lem:prod}(i) with $p=a-1$ and
$q=b-1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:test}]
One direction is Theorem~\ref{thm:chow}. For the other, suppose the $n-2$ displayed
inequalities hold but $T\not\le_{\St}U$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:r12} any index at which the
two right-depth vectors differ satisfies $i\ge3$, so we may choose such an $i$ with
$r_i(T)>r_i(U)$; write
$s=r_i(U)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold} with $K=F_{n-2}(2)$ gives $2^{M_n}>M_n^{\,K}$, and
$K_i(U)\le K$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:Kbound}, so $2^{M_n}>M_n^{\,K_i(U)}$. The chain in the
proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} now runs verbatim with $M_n$ in place of $M$:
\[
  \ev_3(T)(\vec a^{(i)})\ \ge\ F_{s+1}(M_n)\ =\ F_s\bigl(2^{M_n}\bigr)
  \ >\ F_s\bigl(M_n^{\,K_i(U)}\bigr)\ \ge\ \ev_3(U)(\vec a^{(i)}),
\]
contradicting the $i$th displayed inequality.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:notleast}
We do not claim $M_n$ is the least threshold that works. For $n=4$ it is
$M_4=2\cdot16^{2}=512$; for $n=5$ it is $2\cdot65536^{2}=8589934592$.
\end{remark}

\part*{Part II. Up the hierarchy: Tamari and Stanley orders}
\section{Rank four}\label{sec:rank4}

At rank $4$ we know of no identity that would play the role of $(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$,
and a different argument is required.
The proof below is by induction on $z$; the base case $z=1$ must be treated
separately, since the inductive hypothesis at $z=0$ is too weak to survive the step.

\begin{lemma}[Squaring]\label{lem:sq}
If $x\ge2$ and $m\ge1$ then $(x\tw m)^{2}\le x\tw(m+1)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Induction on $m$. For $m=1$ the claim is $x^{2}\le x^{x}$, which holds as $x\ge2$.
For the step put $t=x\tw m$; then
$(x\tw(m+1))^{2}=(x^{t})^{2}=x^{2t}\le x^{(x^{t})}=x\tw(m+2)$,
where $2t\le 2^{t}\le x^{t}$ uses $t\ge1$ and $x\ge2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:sq}
The direct inequality $t^{2}\le x^{t}$ is \emph{false} in general --- take $x=2$,
$t=3$, where $t^{2}=9>8=x^{t}$ --- so the inductive formulation in Lemma~\ref{lem:sq}
is essential.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:add-two}
If $y\ge2$ and $m\ge2$ then $m+2\le y^{m}$. If moreover $m\ge3$ then $m+3\le y^{m}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to use $y^{m}\ge2^{m}$. From $2^{2}=4=2+2$ the bound $2^{m}\ge m+2$
propagates, because $2(m+2)\ge(m+1)+2$; from $2^{3}=8\ge3+3$ the bound
$2^{m}\ge m+3$ propagates, because $2(m+3)\ge(m+1)+3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:B}]
Write $y=y'+1$ with $y'\ge1$ and induct on $z$.
For $z=0$ the left side is $1$ and the right side is $x\ge1$.
For $z=1$ both sides equal $x\tw y$.
Assume $z\ge1$ and set $m:=y\tw z$, so $m\ge y\ge2$ and $y'\le m$.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:add-two} we have $y^{m}\ge m+2$, so $y^{m}=k+1$ with $k\ge m+1$.
Writing $A:=x\tw y=x^{(x\tw y')}$ we compute
\begin{align*}
  (x\tw y)\tw(z+1)
  &= A^{\,(x\tw y)\tw z}
   \ \le\ A^{\,x\tw m}
   \ =\ \bigl(x^{x\tw y'}\bigr)^{x\tw m}
   \ =\ x^{(x\tw y')(x\tw m)}\\
  &\le x^{(x\tw m)^{2}}
   \le x^{\,x\tw(m+1)}
   \le x^{\,x\tw k}
   = x\tw(k+1)
   = x\tw\bigl(y\tw(z+1)\bigr),
\end{align*}
using the inductive hypothesis, $x\tw y'\le x\tw m$, Lemma~\ref{lem:sq}, and
$m+1\le k$. This proves the inequality.

For strictness, note that the penultimate step is strict as soon as $m\ge3$: then
$y^{m}\ge m+3$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:add-two}, hence $m+1<k$ and $x\tw(m+1)<x\tw k$.
Now $m=y\tw z\ge3$ holds whenever $z\ge2$ (then $m\ge4$), and also when $z=1$ and
$y\ge3$ (then $m=y$). This gives strictness at $z+1$ for every $z+1\ge2$ except
$(y,z+1)=(2,2)$, which we compute directly:
\[
  (x\tw2)\tw2=(x^{x})^{x^{x}}=x^{\,x\cdot x^{x}}=x^{\,x^{x+1}},
  \qquad
  x\tw(2\tw2)=x\tw4=x^{\,x^{x^{x}}},
\]
and $x+1<x^{x}$ for $x\ge2$. Hence the inequality is strict for every $z\ge2$.
\end{proof}
\section{The dichotomy}\label{sec:dicho}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:C}]
Order-preservation at every rank $r\ge3$ is Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari}.
Non-strictness at rank~$3$ is Example~\ref{ex:collide}. Strictness at rank~$4$ is
Theorem~\ref{thm:B}; for general $r\ge4$ it is Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover},
which is proved in Section~\ref{sec:general} and rests on
Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3). (The present section proves only the rank-$4$ case, so that
the dichotomy is available before the general induction.)
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari}]
Induction on the derivation of the cover. At the root the claim is
Theorem~\ref{thm:D} instantiated at the values of the three subtrees, which are all
$\ge2$ because $a\ge2$. If the rotation takes place inside the left subtree, one uses
monotonicity of $\HH_r(\cdot,v)$ in its first argument; inside the right subtree,
monotonicity of $\HH_r(u,\cdot)$ in its second argument, valid for $u\ge2$. Both
monotonicity statements hold for every $r\ge1$ and are proved by a double induction
on the rank and on the argument. The formula for the right comb is immediate from
$\HH_{r+1}(a,n+1)=\HH_r(a,\HH_{r+1}(a,n))$ together with $\HH_{r+1}(a,1)=a$.
\end{proof}

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. At rank $3$ the identity
$(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$ turns \eqref{eq:HT} into $yz\le y^{z}$, an inequality which
is \emph{tight}: it becomes an equality at $(y,z)=(2,2)$. Each of the three
coincidences in Example~\ref{ex:collide} is an instance of this one equality, applied
at a node whose two lower subtrees both evaluate to $2$: the cover
$((aa)a)a\lessdot(aa)(aa)$ is the root rotation with $(x,y,z)=(4,2,2)$, and the pairs
$((aa)a)a,\,(a(aa))a$ and $a((aa)a),\,a(a(aa))$ differ only in a subtree
$(aa)a=a(aa)=16$, which is the root rotation with $(x,y,z)=(2,2,2)$. At rank~$4$
Theorem~\ref{thm:B} shows that no equality with $z\ge2$ occurs at all, and the gap is
already visible at the smallest instance: for $x=y=z=2$ the two sides are $256$ and
$65536$.
\section{General rank}\label{sec:general}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm:D}. The engine is the following inequality; write
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:SUM}
  \HH_{r-1}\bigl(\HH_r(x,a),\HH_r(x,b)\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r(x,\,a+b).
  \tag{SUM$_r$}
\end{equation*}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:reduction}
Let $r\ge3$ and $x\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If \textup{(HT$_{r-1}$)} holds, then \textup{(SUM$_r$)} holds for all $a,b\ge1$.
\item If \textup{(SUM$_r$)} holds and $u+v\le\HH_{r-1}(u,v)$ for all $u,v\ge2$,
then \textup{(HT$_r$)} holds for all $y\ge2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(1) Induct on $a$. For $a=1$ both sides are equal:
\[
\HH_{r-1}\bigl(\HH_r(x,1),\HH_r(x,b)\bigr)
  =\HH_{r-1}\bigl(x,\HH_r(x,b)\bigr)=\HH_r(x,b+1).
\]
For the step, $\HH_r(x,a+1)=\HH_{r-1}(x,\HH_r(x,a))$, so by (HT$_{r-1}$)
\[
\HH_{r-1}\bigl(\HH_{r-1}(x,\HH_r(x,a)),\HH_r(x,b)\bigr)
\le \HH_{r-1}\bigl(x,\HH_{r-1}(\HH_r(x,a),\HH_r(x,b))\bigr),
\]
and the inductive hypothesis bounds the inner term by $\HH_r(x,a+b)$, giving
$\HH_{r-1}(x,\HH_r(x,a+b))=\HH_r(x,a+b+1)$.

(2) Induct on $z$, with $z=0,1$ as in Theorem~\ref{thm:B}. For the step set
$m:=\HH_r(y,z)\ge2$; then
$\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),z+1)=\HH_{r-1}(\HH_r(x,y),\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),z))
\le\HH_{r-1}(\HH_r(x,y),\HH_r(x,m))\le\HH_r(x,y+m)\le\HH_r(x,\HH_{r-1}(y,m))
=\HH_r(x,\HH_r(y,z+1))$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:D}]
Induction on $r$. The base case $r=3$ is Theorem~\ref{thm:A}. For the step, the
inductive hypothesis is exactly (HT$_{r-1}$), so Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}(1)
gives (SUM$_r$), and Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}(2) then gives (HT$_r$), the
hypothesis $u+v\le\HH_{r-1}(u,v)$ for $u,v\ge2$ being Lemma~\ref{lem:add}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:add}
For $u,v\ge2$ and $s\ge2$ we have $u+v\le\HH_s(u,v)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$u+v\le uv=\HH_2(u,v)$, and $\HH_2(u,v)\le\HH_s(u,v)$ by monotonicity in the rank.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Strictness}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:addstrict}
For $u,v\ge2$ with $u\ge3$ or $v\ge3$, and $s\ge2$, we have $u+v<\HH_s(u,v)$.
Equality $u+v=\HH_s(u,v)$ occurs exactly at $u=v=2$, where both sides are $4$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$u+v<uv$ holds iff $(u-1)(v-1)>1$, i.e.\ iff $u\ge3$ or $v\ge3$; and
$uv=\HH_2(u,v)\le\HH_s(u,v)$. At $u=v=2$ we have $u+v=4=\HH_2(2,2)=\HH_s(2,2)$ by
Remark~\ref{rem:nontriv}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:E}]
(1) $\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),1)=\HH_r(x,y)=\HH_r(x,\HH_r(y,1))$ since $\HH_r(a,1)=a$.
(2) is Theorem~\ref{thm:A}.
For (3) we induct on $r\ge4$, the base $r=4$ being the strictness clause of
Theorem~\ref{thm:B}. Let $r\ge5$ and write $z=w+1$ with $w\ge1$, and
$m:=\HH_r(y,w)\ge2$.

If $y\ge3$ or $w\ge2$, then $y\ge3$ or $m\ge4$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:addstrict} makes
the last step of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reduction}(2) strict, and
strictness propagates because $\HH_r(x,\cdot)$ is strictly increasing.

The remaining case is $y=z=2$. Put $A:=\HH_r(x,2)=\HH_{r-1}(x,x)\ge4$. Then
\[
  \HH_r(\HH_r(x,2),2)=\HH_{r-1}(A,A),
  \qquad
  \HH_r(x,\HH_r(2,2))=\HH_r(x,4)=\HH_{r-1}\bigl(x,\HH_{r-1}(x,A)\bigr),
\]
using $\HH_r(2,2)=4$ from Remark~\ref{rem:nontriv}. Since $A=\HH_{r-1}(x,x)$, the
required inequality
\[
\HH_{r-1}\bigl(\HH_{r-1}(x,x),A\bigr)<\HH_{r-1}\bigl(x,\HH_{r-1}(x,A)\bigr)
\]
is the inductive hypothesis at rank $r-1$ applied to $(x,x,A)$, and $A\ge2$.
\end{proof}

We can now upgrade Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari} to its strict form.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover}]
Induction on the derivation of the cover, exactly as for
Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari} but with every inequality replaced by its strict
counterpart. At the root the claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3) instantiated at the
values of the three subtrees, which are all $\ge2$ because $a\ge2$
(so in particular the hypothesis $z\ge2$ of that item is met). If the rotation takes
place inside the left subtree, one needs
\[
  2\le u<u' \implies \HH_r(u,v)<\HH_r(u',v) \qquad (v\ge1),
\]
the strict form of monotonicity in the first argument; if it takes place inside the
right subtree, one needs
\[
  1\le v<v' \implies \HH_r(u,v)<\HH_r(u,v') \qquad (u\ge2,\ r\ge2),
\]
the strict form of monotonicity in the second argument. Both are proved by the same
double induction on the rank and on the argument as their non-strict counterparts,
and both apply here because every subtree value is $\ge2$.
\end{proof}

The auxiliary facts used above --- monotonicity of $\HH_r$ in each argument,
monotonicity in the rank, and $n+1\le\HH_r(x,n)$ for $x\ge2$, $r\ge2$,
$n\ge1$ --- are proved from the recursion in Appendix~\ref{app:elementary}. The
corresponding inductions are also part of the formalisation.

The hypothesis $y\ge2$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:D} is sharp, in the following precise
sense: for every $r\ge3$ and all $x,z\ge2$, taking $y=1$ reverses the inequality.
Indeed $\HH_r(x,1)=x$, so the left side is $\HH_r(x,z)$, whereas $\HH_r(1,z)=1$ makes
the right side $\HH_r(x,1)=x$, and $\HH_r(x,z)>x$ because $z\ge2$. The restriction to
$x,z\ge2$ is necessary and not an artefact: for $x=1$ both sides equal $1$, since
$\HH_r(1,\cdot)=1$; for $z=1$ they are equal by Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(1); and for $z=0$
the left side is $1$ and the right side is $x$, so the original inequality still holds.
For $x=0$ the operations degenerate.
\section{Arbitrary leaf values}\label{sec:varleaf}

Nothing in Part~II so far has used the fact that the leaves all carry the same value. The local
law \eqref{eq:HT}, and its strict form, are statements about three independent
arguments; only the evaluation $\ev_{r,a}$ was tied to a single label. Removing that
restriction costs nothing and gives a strictly stronger statement, which is moreover
the one that the discussion recalled in Section~\ref{sec:related} was about.


\begin{theorem}[Arbitrary leaf values]\label{thm:varleaf}
Let $T$ be a labelled tree all of whose leaf labels are $\ge2$, and let $T\lessdot U$
be a Tamari cover. Then
\[
  \ev_r(T)\le\ev_r(U)\quad(r\ge3),
  \qquad
  \ev_r(T)<\ev_r(U)\quad(r\ge4),
\]
and the same holds along the Tamari order itself, that is along the reflexive
transitive closure of the covering relation in the first case and its transitive
closure in the second.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Rotation does not change the multiset of leaf labels, so the hypothesis is preserved
along a cover, and every subtree of $T$ evaluates to at least $2$ at every rank
$r\ge2$ by induction on the tree. The induction on the derivation of the cover is then
the one already used for Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover}: at the root the claim is Theorem~\ref{thm:D}
respectively Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3), instantiated at the three subtree values, which
are independent of one another and all $\ge2$; inside the left subtree one uses
monotonicity, respectively strict monotonicity, of $\HH_r(\cdot,v)$ in its first
argument, and inside the right subtree the corresponding statement for
$\HH_r(u,\cdot)$. The passage to the closures is by induction on the length of the
chain.
\end{proof}

We can now say exactly when a cover fails to be strict. At rank $3$ the only
non-trivial equality of \eqref{eq:HT} is $(y,z)=(2,2)$, and in a labelled tree whose
labels are all $\ge2$ an \emph{internal} node evaluates to at least
$\HH_r(2,2)=4$; so a subtree evaluates to $2$ exactly when it is a leaf labelled $2$.

\begin{corollary}[Equality along a cover]\label{cor:coveq}
Let all labels be $\ge2$ and consider the rotation
$((A\!\cdot\!B)\!\cdot\!C)\to(A\!\cdot\!(B\!\cdot\!C))$ at rank~$3$. Its two sides are
equal if and only if $B$ and $C$ are both leaves labelled~$2$. Consequently, for a
single repeated label $a$, every cover is strict unless $(r,a)=(3,2)$; and for
$(r,a)=(3,2)$ equality does occur, as Example~\ref{ex:collide} shows.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:A} the two sides agree exactly when $\ev_3(C)=1$ or
$(\ev_3(B),\ev_3(C))=(2,2)$. The first is impossible because every label is $\ge2$. For the
second, a subtree evaluates to $2$ only if it is a leaf labelled~$2$, by the remark
above. In a surrounding context each ancestor applies a strictly increasing function of
the changed subtree value (Appendix~\ref{app:elementary}), so equality of the two whole
trees is equivalent to equality at the rotation. For a single label $a$: if $a\ge3$ no leaf is labelled~$2$, so no cover at
rank~$3$ is an equality, and from rank~$4$ on Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover} applies.
\end{proof}

Thus the collapse at rank $3$ is not merely rare but completely located: it happens at
exactly one local configuration, two leaves labelled $2$ being rotated past each other,
and from rank $4$ on it does not happen at all.

Setting every label equal to $a$ recovers Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover}; the formalisation carries out this specialisation
explicitly, so the earlier statements are corollaries rather than separate results.

Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} is what extends the rank-$3$ discussion of
Section~\ref{sec:related} to the whole hierarchy. At rank $3$ it is subsumed by
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, since Tamari comparability implies Stanley comparability
(Remark~\ref{rem:fit}); what it contributes is the ranks $r\ge4$, where the separation
argument of Part~I has no known analogue.
\section{The Stanley order at every rank}\label{sec:stanleyrank}

Section~\ref{sec:varleaf} shows that evaluation respects the \emph{Tamari} order at
every rank. In fact more is true: it respects the \emph{Stanley} order, which is
strictly larger for $n\ge4$. The proof needs the description of the covering relation, the
\emph{non-strict} nested upper bound stated by Leonardis, d'Atri and
Caldarola~\cite[Lemma~4.8]{LdC},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nest}
  \HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,y),z\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r(x,y+z)
  \qquad(r\ge4,\ x\ge2,\ y,z\ge1),
\end{equation}
and Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3), which is proved in Section~\ref{sec:general}. The base
$x=2$ is needed, because a leaf may carry the label $2$, and the published proof
of~\cite[Lemma~4.8]{LdC} does not treat it; Lemma~\ref{lem:nest} in
Appendix~\ref{app:nest} therefore proves \eqref{eq:nest}, in strict form, for every base
$x\ge2$. The proof below does \emph{not} need the strict form;
Remark~\ref{rem:saistrict} says what that stronger form buys.

The strictness comes from a gap that the hierarchy opens in its first argument.

\begin{lemma}[A two-unit gap in the base]\label{lem:gap}
For $r\ge3$ and $t,d\ge2$ we have $\HH_r(t+1,d)\ge\HH_r(t,d)+2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $u=\HH_r(t,d-1)$ and $v=\HH_r(t+1,d-1)$. Since $d-1\ge1$ we have $v>u\ge2$, both by
monotonicity in the base and by $\HH_r(t,1)=t$. As $r-1\ge2$, the two comparisons
\[
  \HH_{r-1}(t,u)\ <\ \HH_{r-1}(t,v)\ <\ \HH_{r-1}(t+1,v)
\]
are strict, the first by monotonicity in the second argument and the second by
monotonicity in the first. Their outer terms are $\HH_r(t,d)$ and $\HH_r(t+1,d)$. Two
strict inequalities between integers force a difference of at least $2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The left spine widens the gap]\label{lem:Ggap}
Let $r\ge3$, let $d_1,\dots,d_m$ be integers $\ge2$ with $m\ge1$, and put
$G(t)=\bigl(\cdots((t*d_1)*d_2)\cdots*d_m\bigr)$ with $x*y=\HH_r(x,y)$. Then
$G(t+1)\ge G(t)+2$ for every $t\ge2$, and hence $G(t+b)\ge G(t)+2b$ for every $b\ge0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The innermost map $t\mapsto t*d_1$ increases by at least $2$ when $t$ does by $1$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}. If $f$ is a strictly increasing map of the integers $\ge2$ and
$A\ge B+2$, then $f(B)<f(B+1)<f(B+2)\le f(A)$, so $f(A)\ge f(B)+2$; each further map
$t\mapsto t*d_j$ is such an $f$, so the gap survives the composition. Telescoping gives
the second statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Stanley monotonicity at every rank]\label{thm:strank}
Let $r\ge4$ and let $T,U\in\Tam_n$ be labelled trees with the same labels, all $\ge2$.
If $T<_{\St}U$ then $\ev_r(T)<\ev_r(U)$. Consequently
$\le_{\St}\ \subseteq\ \le_{\ev}$ at every rank $r\ge4$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:stcover} it suffices to treat a single cover, and by
Lemma~\ref{lem:localcover} we may take
$T=((A\cdot B)\cdot L_{\mathbf D}(c))$ and $U=(A\cdot L_{\mathbf D}((B\cdot c)))$ inside
a context. Write $x*y=\HH_r(x,y)$, let $a,b$ be the values of $A,B$, let $c$ also denote
the value of the leftmost leaf, let $d_j$ be the value of $D_j$, and set
\[
  G(t)=\bigl(\cdots((t*d_1)*d_2)\cdots *d_m\bigr),\qquad G(t)=t \text{ if } m=0 .
\]
All of $a,b,c,d_j$ are $\ge2$, and $\ev_r(T)=(a*b)*G(c)$, $\ev_r(U)=a*G(b*c)$.

If $m=0$ then $G$ is the identity and the two values are $(a*b)*c$ and $a*(b*c)$, so the
assertion is exactly the strict form of the semi-associative law,
Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3), applied at $(a,b,c)$ with $c\ge2$.

Suppose $m\ge1$. Since $\HH_r$ dominates $\HH_2$ on arguments $\ge2$ we have
$b*c\ge bc\ge b+c$, the second because $(b-1)(c-1)\ge1$; and $G$ is strictly increasing,
so Lemma~\ref{lem:Ggap} gives
\[
  G(b*c)\ \ge\ G(b+c)\ \ge\ G(c)+2b\ >\ G(c)+b .
\]
Now \eqref{eq:nest} bounds the left-hand value and strict monotonicity of
$\HH_r(a,\cdot)$ turns that last strict inequality into the conclusion:
\[
  \ev_r(T)=(a*b)*G(c)\ \le\ a*\bigl(b+G(c)\bigr)\ <\ a*G(b*c)=\ev_r(U).
\]
Since $\HH_r$ is strictly increasing in each argument on values $\ge2$, the strict
inequality propagates through the surrounding context, and chaining covers gives the
theorem.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:saistrict}
The case $m=0$ is where the argument has to be split, and the reason is visible at
$b=c=2$: there $b*c=4=b+c$, so $G(b*c)=G(b+c)$, and with $G$ the identity the chain
above closes with an equality rather than a strict inequality. For $m\ge1$ the gap of
Lemma~\ref{lem:Ggap} supplies the missing unit; for $m=0$ the cover is a Tamari
rotation and Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3) supplies it. With the \emph{strict} form of
\eqref{eq:nest} --- Saibian's Theorem~I~\cite{Sai}, which reads
$\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),z)<\HH_r(x,y+z)$ in the present notation and is proved here as
Lemma~\ref{lem:nest} --- the split is unnecessary, since the first inequality above is
then already strict. We print the route through the non-strict bound because it shows
how little the Stanley covering relation needs; the Lean development follows the shorter
route (Section~\ref{sec:formal}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:strankcontains}
Theorem~\ref{thm:strank} contains Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} for $r\ge4$, because the
Tamari order is contained in the Stanley order. It also removes one possibility from
Section~\ref{sec:open}: at rank $r\ge4$ the evaluation order cannot lie strictly between
the Tamari and the Stanley orders. What remains open there is the reverse inclusion
$\le_{\ev}\subseteq\le_{\St}$, for which the argument of Part~I is unavailable: that
argument rests on the collapsing law $(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$ through
Lemma~\ref{lem:absorb}, and no analogue is known for tetration.
\end{remark}

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:comp}

The rank-three results of Part~I were checked by exact integer computation before and
after they were proved. Trees were enumerated, right-depth vectors and Dyck words computed
independently, and the two descriptions of the Stanley order compared: they agree on all
$203{,}455$ ordered pairs for $2\le n\le8$. As an external check, the right-depth vectors for $n=5$
were shifted by $c_j=r_{j+1}-1$ and compared with the fourteen depth sequences printed
in Fig.~41 of~\cite{Knuth}: the two sets are equal. The bounds of
Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}, the threshold of Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold} and the basic
inequalities of Lemma~\ref{lem:basic} were verified on the ranges where exact integers
remain feasible, with no exceptions.

Independently, the order was computed for $n\le6$ by evaluating all trees at several
thousand assignments and comparing logarithms in high precision. That last computation
is experimental only: floating-point logarithms carry no certificate for the sign of a
comparison, and it should be read as evidence rather than proof. Its outcome is that
the number of comparable pairs agrees with the number of Stanley-comparable pairs ($9$ at $n=4$, $70$ at $n=5$),
and all $309$ Stanley-incomparable pairs at $n=6$ were separated in both directions.
The shape of that agreement is worth stating, but so is its limit. For a finite set $A$
of assignments write $T\le_A U$ when the comparison holds at every member of $A$; then
$\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}\subseteq\le_A$, the second inclusion because a finite
probe can only \emph{enlarge} the relation. So if $\le_A$ were computed with certified
comparisons and came out equal to $\le_{\St}$, the three relations would coincide at that
$n$. \textbf{That is not what the floating-point run provides}: an uncertified sign gives
no such guarantee, and the agreement reported above is evidence and nothing more. The
proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} and Corollary~\ref{cor:test} do not use it; the
certified counterpart is Corollary~\ref{cor:test} itself, whose $n-2$ assignments are
exactly such a set $A$.

\begin{example}\label{ex:sep512}
For the pair of Example~\ref{ex:four}, Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} gives the two assignments
explicitly. With leaves $(2,2,2,512)$ one has
$\ev_3\bigl((ab)(cd)\bigr)=2^{2^{513}}$ and $\ev_3\bigl((a(bc))d\bigr)=2^{2048}$; with

\section{Related work, and what we claim}\label{sec:related}

Cs\'ak\'any and Waldhauser~\cite{CsW} introduce the associative spectrum and show
that exponentiation is Catalan: for pairwise distinct prime leaves, distinct
bracketings give distinct values. Their concern is the number of values; the
order in which those values arrange themselves is not addressed: Tamari's
paper~\cite{Tamari} and a paper of Bennett and Birkhoff on Newman lattices appear in
their bibliography only as general references for bracketings, and no order on
bracketings is used. Huang and Lehtonen~\cite{HL} extend the invariant to the
\emph{associative-commutative spectrum} and determine it for exponentiation
(their Proposition 6.1.5): the associative spectrum of exponentiation is $C_{n-1}$
and its ac-spectrum is $n^{n-1}$. Their question is again how many distinct term
operations the bracketings produce; ours is how the resulting values are
\emph{arranged}, and neither the Tamari order nor any statement comparing values along
an order occurs in that line of work. Huang, Mickey and Xu~\cite{HMX} compute the associative
spectrum of a further operation. Earlier, G\"obel and Nederpelt~\cite{GN} counted the
values of the bracketings when every leaf carries the same real number $x>1$: there are
at most as many as there are rooted trees with one edge fewer than the number of leaves,
with a sharper upper bound for $x=2$ in terms of trimmed trees, and, for each number of
leaves, exactly that many for all but countably many $x$ (their Theorems~2.2, 3.1
and~5.1); they, too, count values and do not compare them. Tamari~\cite{Tamari} enumerated the bracketings, and Friedman and
Tamari~\cite{FT} proved that the right-rotation order on them is a finite lattice (the
statement, they note, is already in Tamari's thesis of 1951, without proof) ---
their title already calls the relation a \emph{loi demi-associative}, which is
precisely the law that runs through the whole hyperoperation hierarchy.
Zeilberger~\cite{Zeil} gives a sequent calculus for that law. Gryszka~\cite{Gry} studies the comparison of power towers
by normalising them, which concerns right-associated towers rather than general
bracketings.

\subsection{Where the rank-three case comes from, and what we add}

It is important to say clearly what part of Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari} is already
known. In a MathOverflow question of January 2018~\cite{MO}, Spivak treats
exponentiation on the integers $\ge2$ as an ordered magma and orders bracketings by
the \emph{evaluation order}: $E\le F$ when the inequality holds \emph{for every}
assignment of values to the leaves. He asks how this order relates to the height
(Stanley) order on Dyck paths. In the answers, Chow, following a comment of Rubey on the
question, proves that a Stanley cover does \emph{not} decrease the value, the
computation resting on
\[
  B^{CD}=(B^{C})^{D}\ \ge\ (BC)^{D}\ \ge\ BC^{D}
  \qquad (B,C\ge2,\ D\ge1),
\]
and Rubey, independently, reduces the same covering relation to the inequality
$L_2^{(L_1^{xR})}\ge(L_2^{L_1})^{(x^{R})}$, which he leaves unverified. That inequality
is not \eqref{eq:HT} at rank $3$: writing it as
$x'^{(y'^{xR})}\ge(x'^{y'})^{(z')}$ with $z'=x^{R}$, it reduces to \eqref{eq:HT} only
when $xR=x^{R}$, which for $x\ge2$ and $R\ge1$ happens exactly when $R=1$ or
$(x,R)=(2,2)$; outside those it is strictly stronger, being the
inequality attached to a \emph{Stanley} cover rather than a Tamari one.

Since the evaluation order quantifies over \emph{all} leaf assignments it is stronger
than the single-label evaluation $\ev_{3,a}$, and since the Stanley lattice is an
extension of the Tamari lattice, comparability in the Tamari order implies
comparability in the Stanley order. Consequently \textbf{the rank-$3$ monotonicity is
already contained, in a stronger form, in that discussion, and we do not claim it.}

That discussion is, however, explicitly unfinished, and it is confined to rank~$3$.
Chow states that he can ``complete half of the proof'', the half we have just quoted;
the converse half --- that incomparable bracketings can be separated in both
directions by a suitable choice of leaf values --- he leaves open, writing
``I haven't quite figured out how to say this rigorously''. Rubey likewise reduces the
covering relation to the displayed inequality and stops there. In comments under his
answer he suggested that Chow's idea should also give the converse, first by a reduction
to paths that differ in one valley and one peak and then, after Chow cautioned that
reductions for this half need care, by comparing two auxiliary paths that separate at a
single peak, with ``some details to check here''; the sketch was not completed. Neither
treats any rank above~$3$, and neither asks when equality holds.

Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} above takes up the first half at every rank. It says that
for arbitrary leaf values $\ge2$ the evaluation is monotone along the Tamari order at
every rank $r\ge3$, and \emph{strictly} monotone from rank~$4$ on. \textbf{Chow's
converse half is settled at rank~$3$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} of Part~I}; above rank
$3$ it remains open, and Section~\ref{sec:open} says what would be needed.

There is also a body of peer-reviewed work on orders attached to iterated
exponentials, and it is worth saying precisely how it relates to the present question,
because the titles suggest a closer overlap than there is. Brunson~\cite{Bru} fixes
$e\le x_1\le\dots\le x_n$ and, \emph{for each permutation} of the $x_i$, forms the
tower $T(y,z,\dots,w)=y^{z^{\cdots^{w}}}$, in which ``association is always to the
upper right''; the resulting partial order lives on the symmetric group $S_n$.
Brunson's order agrees with the Bruhat order for $n\le4$, up to a duality that depends
on the conventions, and Brunson conjectured properties that the Bruhat order has; that
the two orders nevertheless differ was shown independently, and at about the same time,
by Stembridge~\cite{Ste}, who gave an explicit characterisation of the order that
disproves those conjectures, and by Griggs--Wachs~\cite{GW}, whose counterexample at
$n=5$ also disproves Brunson's Conjectures~A and~B. (The independence and the
near-simultaneity are stated by Griggs and Wachs themselves.)
Griggs~\cite{Gri} studies the poset of towers over the two-letter alphabet
$\{a,b\}^n$, the tower again being ``evaluated in the conventional way, from top
down''.

In every one of these the \emph{bracketing is fixed} --- it is the right comb --- and
what varies is the assignment of entries to its leaves. In the single-label problem of
Sections~\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:general} the situation is exactly the reverse: the
entries are fixed, all equal to $a$, and what varies is the bracketing. (Section~%
\ref{sec:varleaf} then lets the entries vary as well, but still over \emph{all}
bracketings rather than over the right comb alone.) The two families of questions are therefore orthogonal, and none of these
papers contains the inequality studied here. (None of \cite{Bru,Gri,GW} contains an
occurrence of the words \emph{bracketing}, \emph{Tamari}, \emph{binary tree},
\emph{Catalan}, \emph{rotation} or \emph{Dyck}.)

\subsection{Nested upper bounds in the hyperoperation literature}

The inequality \eqref{eq:HT} compares a nesting on the left with a nesting on the
right. A \emph{stronger} upper bound, in which the right-hand side carries the sum
$y+z$ --- which is at most $\HH_r(y,z)$ --- in place of $\HH_r(y,z)$, has been recorded
before, and at every rank.
Leonardis, d'Atri and Caldarola~\cite{LdC} state, as their Lemma~4.8,
\[
  (A\uparrow^{k}B)\uparrow^{k}C\ \le\ A\uparrow^{k}(B+C)
  \qquad(k\ge2),
\]
with the tetration case $k=2$ isolated as their Lemma~4.6; in the notation of
Section~\ref{sec:prelim} this is $\HH_r(\HH_r(x,y),z)\le\HH_r(x,y+z)$ for $r\ge4$. In
the published version the proof of Lemma~4.8 assumes $A\ge3$ and leaves the case $A=2$
to the reader; Appendix~\ref{app:nest} says why that case needs an argument, and gives
one.
In an earlier preprint, Saibian~\cite{Sai} states the same comparison with a
\emph{strict} inequality (his Theorem~I), and proves it: he sets down nine axioms,
derives from them that each $\HH_r(b,\cdot)$ is strictly increasing and exceeds its
argument and that each rank dominates the one below, proves the tetration case
$(b\uparrow\uparrow m)\uparrow\uparrow n<b\uparrow\uparrow(m+n)$ for $b>1$, and lifts it
to every higher rank by induction.

This matters for the present paper, because \eqref{eq:HT} follows from either of them
in two lines. For positive integers $y,z\ge2$ one has $y+z\le yz\le\HH_r(y,z)$, and
$\HH_r(x,\cdot)$ is nondecreasing, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fromnested}
  \HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,y),z\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r(x,y+z)\ \le\ \HH_r\bigl(x,\HH_r(y,z)\bigr).
\end{equation}
With Saibian's strict form the first inequality is strict, so \eqref{eq:fromnested}
already yields the strictness of Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3) on the range $z\ge2$; the
remaining boundaries $z=0$ and $z=1$ are immediate from the definition. \textbf{We
therefore do not claim Theorem~\ref{thm:D}, nor the high-rank half of
Theorem~\ref{thm:E}, as new arithmetic}; our proofs in Section~\ref{sec:general} are
independent, but the results are not.

One caveat about two of these sources should be stated plainly: we cite
Saibian~\cite{Sai} in its viXra version and Barrett~\cite{Bar} in its Preprints.org
version, rather than as refereed journal articles.
We have read both in the original and checked the statements we quote --- Saibian's
Theorem~I and Barrett's Theorems~6.5 and~6.6. Neither source is a bare announcement:
each carries a proof, and we have followed the structure of Saibian's, although we have
not audited the whole of his thirty-two pages. \textbf{No result of this paper rests on
either of them.} They are cited for attribution: the arithmetic they contain is proved
here as well, in Section~\ref{sec:general} and Appendix~\ref{app:nest}, and a reader who
declines to rely on preprints should read those as the proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:D},
Theorem~\ref{thm:E} and Lemma~\ref{lem:nest} rather than as second proofs of them.
\textbf{Nor does any result rest on an outside proof of a nested bound.} The one such
bound that Theorem~\ref{thm:strank} uses is the non-strict inequality~\eqref{eq:nest}
of Leonardis, d'Atri and Caldarola~\cite{LdC}, which appeared in a refereed journal; but
it is used at the base $2$, which their published proof does not treat, and so it is
proved here as Lemma~\ref{lem:nest}. In the Lean development the older, shorter
derivation survives, through the strict form of that lemma, which is proved there as
well.

The equality question has also been addressed directly. Barrett~\cite{Bar}, in a
preprint on factorisation under hyperoperations, proves as his Theorem~6.5 that
$(a^{b})^{c}=a^{(b^{c})}$ with $a,b,c>1$ forces $b=c=2$; this is exactly the
non-trivial rank-$3$ equality of Theorem~\ref{thm:A}. His Theorem~6.6 asserts that
for $n\ge4$ and $a>1$, equality in $(a[n]b)[n]c=a[n](b[n]c)$ forces $b=c=1$. Read as a
statement on its own this is defective at the boundary: taking $c=1$ gives
\[
  \HH_n\bigl(\HH_n(a,b),1\bigr)=\HH_n(a,b)=\HH_n\bigl(a,\HH_n(b,1)\bigr)
\]
for all $a,b\ge2$ and $n\ge2$, so that $(a,b,c,n)=(2,2,1,4)$ is an equality with
$b=2$; both sides equal $4$. (The corollary invoked in that proof requires all four
compared arguments to exceed $1$, which fails at $c=1$.)

\textbf{This is not a refutation of Barrett's result.} The opening of his
\S6.2 says explicitly that the discussion will ``also ignore cases where any of the
operands is 0 or 1'', and the sentence closing the proof of Theorem~6.6 returns to the
conclusion that there is no non-trivial associativity for $n\ge4$ with $a,b,c>1$. On
that domain his Theorem~6.6 \emph{is} Theorem~\ref{thm:E}(3). What we add is not a
correction of his mathematics but a single statement that carries its own boundary
conditions: Theorem~\ref{thm:E} says, for $x,y\ge2$ and $r\ge4$, that equality holds
if and only if $z=1$, and it settles $z=0$ as well. The hypotheses $x,y\ge2$ are needed:
at $r=4$, $x=2$, $y=0$, $z=3$ both sides are $1$.

A remark on the minimiser is also in order. The left comb at rank~$3$ evaluates to
$\ev_{3,a}(L_n)=a^{a^{\,n-1}}$, and this is the operation known in the
hyperoperation literature as the \emph{lower} hyperoperation $\mathrm{lower}_4$,
obtained by iterating exponentiation to the left rather than to the right; see
Andonov~\cite{And}, who places it in the lower hyperoperation sequence. The
identification is with the rank-$3$ left comb specifically: the left-iterated
operations of higher rank are not in general the left combs of the corresponding
rank, so we do not extend the name upwards.

\subsection{The associative spectrum, and the two uses of right depth}

The bracketings of a word under a fixed binary operation have been studied as the
\emph{associative spectrum}, the sequence counting how many distinct term operations
they induce. Cs\'ak\'any and Waldhauser~\cite{CsW} introduced the invariant and computed
it for exponentiation in $3.4$, obtaining the Catalan numbers; Lehtonen and
Waldhauser~\cite{LW} determine the spectra of graph algebras; Huang and
Lehtonen~\cite{HL} introduce the associative-commutative spectrum and compute it for
exponentiation in their Proposition~6.1.5. That line of work counts how many operations
the bracketings produce; the present question is how the resulting values are
\emph{arranged}. The two are different, and the difference is exactly the gap this paper
closes: knowing that two bracketings induce different functions says nothing about which
one is the larger at a given assignment, let alone at every assignment.

A separate body of work orders \emph{towers} rather than bracketings.
Brunson~\cite{Bru} fixes the right-nested bracketing and permutes the entries;
Stembridge~\cite{Ste} and Griggs--Wachs~\cite{GW} compare the
resulting order with the Bruhat order. There the bracketing is fixed and the entries
move; here the entries are arbitrary and the bracketing moves.

\subsection{What we do claim}

Not finding a result is not a proof that it is new, so we state the claim in pieces,
and we begin with what is \emph{not} claimed.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Not claimed, Part~I.} The inclusion $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$ at
rank three is due to Chow~\cite{MO}; Rubey independently reduced it to a single
inequality. We reprove it as
Theorem~\ref{thm:chow}, by a different and shorter argument, only so that the paper is
self-contained; the result is his. The coordinate presentation of
Proposition~\ref{prop:coord} is known: Knuth defines the Stanley lattice by depth
coordinates~\cite{Knuth}, Lehtonen and Waldhauser identify DFS depth with right
depth~\cite{LW}, and $2.8$ of~\cite{CsW} proves that right-depth sequences determine a
bracketing. The reading of $r_i$ as the level of a leaf in the resulting tower, which is
the qualitative content of Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}, is $3.4$ of~\cite{CsW}. The
idea of enlarging one variable to force a comparison is Chow's, stated in the same
answer~\cite{MO}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Not claimed, Part~II.} The rank-$3$ monotonicity is known~\cite{MO}.
The local law \eqref{eq:HT} at every rank, and its strictness for $r\ge4$, follow in two
lines from the nested upper bounds of Leonardis--d'Atri--Caldarola~\cite{LdC} and
Saibian~\cite{Sai} by \eqref{eq:fromnested}; Theorem~\ref{thm:D} and parts~(1), (3), (4)
of Theorem~\ref{thm:E} are therefore \emph{not} new arithmetic, and our independent
proofs in Section~\ref{sec:general} are offered as proofs, not as first occurrences.
The nested bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:nest} is theirs as well; we prove it only because the
published proof of~\cite[Lemma~4.8]{LdC} does not treat the base~$2$. The non-trivial
rank-$3$ equality $(y,z)=(2,2)$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:A} is Barrett's
Theorem~6.5~\cite{Bar}; its instance $x=y=z=2$, the relation $(2^2)^2=2^{(2^2)}$, is the
one in view of which G\"obel and Nederpelt~\cite{GN} expect the base~$2$ to behave
differently.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{What we offer.} First, and principally, the converse inclusion, and
hence Theorem~\ref{thm:main}: at rank~$3$ the evaluation order is exactly the Stanley
order, which settles the half of~\cite{MO} that was left open. Second, the quantitative
form of that separation, Theorem~\ref{thm:sep}, in which the leaf to enlarge, the value
to put there, and the fact that all other leaves may remain at $2$ are determined
explicitly, together with the finite test family of Corollary~\ref{cor:test}. Third, the
equality question settled by a single statement carrying its own boundary conditions:
Theorem~\ref{thm:E} classifies equality at \emph{every} rank and at every boundary,
where the statement of Barrett's Theorem~6.6 taken on its own is defective at $z=1$
although his section excludes that case by hypothesis. Fourth, Theorem~\ref{thm:strank}: the previously stated \emph{non-strict} nested bound,
together with the two-unit gap of Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}, is strong enough to carry
the Stanley covering relation, so evaluation is strictly Stanley-monotone at every rank
$r\ge4$ --- which settles one of the two implications of
Conjecture~\ref{conj:higher} at every rank above $3$. Fifth, the organisation of the
rest of that arithmetic as an order-theoretic statement about the Tamari lattice:
Proposition~\ref{prop:tamari}, Theorem~\ref{thm:strictcover} and Corollary~\ref{cor:C}
locate the failure of strictness at the single pair $(r,a)=(3,2)$ and show that from
rank~$4$ on the evaluation is strictly increasing along every cover, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:varleaf} gives the cover version for arbitrary leaf values $\ge2$.
Sixth, the Lean formalisation, whose extent and whose three limits are set out in
Section~\ref{sec:formal}: it covers the forward implication of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} end to end, both of the ingredients of the converse, and
Lemmas~\ref{lem:stcover} and~\ref{lem:localcover} with $2.8$ of~\cite{CsW}.

Within the literature we have searched we have not found the converse inclusion, the
quantitative separation, the Tamari formulation, the complete equality classification,
or an equivalent formalisation; we do not convert that into a claim of first occurrence.
We should add that the two ingredients of Part~I that we take as known were found late,
in the course of checking the literature for this paper, and that they are close to the
result: $2.8$ and $3.4$ of~\cite{CsW} together give the injectivity of $r$ and the
interpretation of $r_i$ as a level in the tower, which is most of the apparatus used in
Sections~\ref{sec:coord}--\ref{sec:sep}. What they do not give is any comparison of
magnitudes: substituting distinct primes and appealing to unique factorisation shows
that two values differ, never which is larger. We should also say that the qualitative
converse --- that \emph{some} sufficiently large value at leaf $i$ separates --- can be
reached from their apparatus by a short asymptotic argument: fixing every other leaf at
$2$ and letting $t\to\infty$, an induction along the path to the root gives
$\log^{\circ h}v_{T,i}(t)\sim Ct^{\alpha}$ with $h=r_i(T)$ and $C,\alpha>0$, so a tree
with the larger right depth wins for all large $t$. That argument gives no threshold.
What Theorem~\ref{thm:sep} and Corollary~\ref{cor:test} add is the threshold and the
uniformity: which leaf, what value, and a finite family of assignments fixed in advance.

\section{What remains open}\label{sec:open}

The two parts fill two corners of a square whose axes are the rank and the order.

\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
 & \textbf{Tamari} & \textbf{Stanley}\\[2pt]
rank $3$ & $\le_{\Tam}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$, equality classified & $\le_{\St}=\le_{\ev}$
   (Theorem~\ref{thm:main})\\
rank $\ge4$ & $\le_{\Tam}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$, strict on covers &
   $\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:strank});\\
 & & converse \textbf{open}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\medskip

\noindent The entries in the first column are containments only. We have not proved
that either is proper directly: strict increase of the \emph{values} along a cover does
not make the containment of \emph{relations} strict. Properness does follow, for
$n\ge4$ and at \emph{every} rank, from the second column together with
$\le_{\Tam}\subsetneq\le_{\St}$; for $n\le3$ the two orders coincide, since there are
at most two trees and they are Tamari-comparable.

What is missing is one implication, the same one at every rank above $3$.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:higher}
For every $r\ge4$ the evaluation order $\le_{\ev}$ on $\Tam_n$, taken over all leaf
assignments with values $\ge2$, is the Stanley order.
\end{conjecture}

At rank~$3$ the same statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
One half of Conjecture~\ref{conj:higher} is Theorem~\ref{thm:strank}: the inclusion
$\le_{\St}\subseteq\le_{\ev}$ holds at every rank $r\ge4$, and in the strict form.
What is missing is the converse $\le_{\ev}\subseteq\le_{\St}$, and the obstruction is
easy to name: at rank $3$ the converse is Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, which rests on
Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}, whose two-sided estimate is built from the collapsing law
$(x^{y})^{z}=x^{yz}$ through the absorption Lemma~\ref{lem:absorb}. No analogue of that
identity is known for tetration, and without it we cannot bound from above the tower
that one enlarged leaf generates.

So at rank $\ge4$ the evaluation order is known to contain $\le_{\St}$ and is therefore
not strictly between $\le_{\Tam}$ and $\le_{\St}$; whether it is exactly $\le_{\St}$ or
strictly larger, we do not know.

\section{Elementary hyperoperation facts}\label{app:elementary}

This appendix collects the elementary inductions used in the body. None of them uses a
semi-associative inequality, so nothing here is circular. Bases are integers $x\ge2$
unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{Growth and monotonicity in the arguments.}
For every $r\ge2$ and $n\ge1$,
\[
 \HH_r(x,1)=x,\qquad \HH_r(x,n)\ge x,\qquad \HH_r(x,n)\ge n+1;
\]
moreover $n\mapsto\HH_r(x,n)$ is strictly increasing for $n\ge0$, and
$x\mapsto\HH_r(x,n)$ is strictly increasing on integer bases $x\ge2$ when $n\ge1$.
These are proved together by induction on $r$. At $r=2$ they follow from
$\HH_2(x,n)=xn$; in particular $xn\ge2n\ge n+1$ for $n\ge1$. Suppose they hold at rank
$r$. At rank $r+1\ge3$ the values at $n=0$ and $n=1$ are $1$ and $x$. For $n\ge1$ put
$t=\HH_{r+1}(x,n)$; then $t\ge x\ge2$, and the hypothesis at rank $r$ gives
\[
 \HH_{r+1}(x,n+1)=\HH_r(x,t)\ \ge\ t+1 .
\]
Induction on $n$ now yields strict increase and both growth bounds at rank $r+1$.

For strict increase in the base, fix $2\le x<x'$ and induct on $n\ge1$. At $n=1$ the
assertion is $x<x'$. If $a=\HH_{r+1}(x,n)<\HH_{r+1}(x',n)=a'$, then $a,a'\ge2$ and
\[
 \HH_r(x,a)\ <\ \HH_r(x,a')\ \le\ \HH_r(x',a'),
\]
by monotonicity in the second argument and in the base at rank $r$; this is the
comparison at $n+1$. Non-strict monotonicity follows by allowing equality throughout.

\begin{corollary}[Propagation through a context]\label{cor:context}
Let $r\ge3$ and let a tree context have all its other leaf values $\ge2$. Then the value
of the whole tree is a strictly increasing function of the value substituted at the hole,
on substituted values $\ge2$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Induct along the path from the hole to the root. Each step applies
$u\mapsto\HH_r(u,v)$ or $u\mapsto\HH_r(v,u)$ for a fixed $v\ge2$, and both are strictly
increasing on arguments $\ge2$ by the above.
\end{proof}

In particular such a context preserves and reflects equality, and preserves strict
inequality; this is what is used in Corollary~\ref{cor:chowcase} and in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:strank}.

\paragraph{Monotonicity in the rank.}
For $r\ge2$, $x\ge2$ and $n\ge1$ we have $\HH_r(x,n)\le\HH_{r+1}(x,n)$. For $r=2$ this
is $xn\le x^{n}$, which is Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow}. For the step, let $r\ge3$ and
assume the comparison between ranks $r-1$ and $r$. At $n=1$ both values are $x$, and if
the comparison holds at $n$ then
\[
 \HH_r(x,n+1)=\HH_{r-1}\bigl(x,\HH_r(x,n)\bigr)
 \ \le\ \HH_r\bigl(x,\HH_r(x,n)\bigr)
 \ \le\ \HH_r\bigl(x,\HH_{r+1}(x,n)\bigr)=\HH_{r+1}(x,n+1),
\]
the first inequality by the induction on the rank at a positive argument and the second
by monotonicity in that argument. In particular $\HH_r(x,n)\ge xn$ for $r\ge2$ and
$n\ge1$, which is the domination used in Section~\ref{sec:stanleyrank}.

\paragraph{The base $1$.}
For $r\ge3$ and $n\ge0$ we have $\HH_r(1,n)=1$. At rank $3$ this is $1^{n}=1$. At higher
ranks, induction on $n$ uses the value $1$ at $n=0$ and $\HH_{r-1}(1,1)=1$ from
Lemma~\ref{lem:conv}. These are the boundary comparisons appealed to in
Section~\ref{sec:general}.

\section{The nested upper bound}\label{app:nest}

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:strank} applies \eqref{eq:nest} with $x$ the value of a
subtree, and that value is $2$ whenever the subtree is a leaf labelled $2$. The bound is
Lemma~4.8 of Leonardis, d'Atri and Caldarola~\cite{LdC}, and in strict form, for bases
$b>1$, it is Theorem~I of Saibian~\cite{Sai}. In the published version of~\cite{LdC} the proof of
Lemma~4.8 assumes $A\ge3$ and leaves the case $A=2$ to the reader; for $k=2$ it rests on
their Lemma~4.6, whose proof ends by passing from a tower built on $A+1$ to one built on
$A$, a step that needs $(A+1)\tw m\le A\tw(m+1)$ with $m=B+C-3$ and already fails at
$A=2$, $m=2$, since $3\tw2=27>16=2\tw3$. The arXiv version of the same paper carries
neither the restriction nor the remark about the reader, but its proof of Lemma~4.8 rests
on the same Lemma~4.6 and takes the same step, so neither printed proof establishes the
base~$2$. We therefore give a complete proof, in the
strict form, for every base $x\ge2$. It uses only Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow} and the
facts of Appendix~\ref{app:elementary}, and it has been formalised
(Section~\ref{sec:formal}).

\begin{lemma}[Nested upper bound]\label{lem:nest}
Let $r\ge4$, $x\ge2$ and $y,z\ge1$. Then
\[
  \HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,y),z\bigr)\ <\ \HH_r(x,y+z).
\]
In particular \eqref{eq:nest} holds.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From Appendix~\ref{app:elementary} we use that $\HH_s(x,n)\ge n+1$ and $\HH_s(x,n)\ge xn$
for $s\ge2$ and $n\ge1$, and that $\HH_s(x,\cdot)$ is increasing. At every rank $s\ge3$
put $W=\HH_s(x,y-1)$ and $T=\HH_s(x,y)$, so that $T=\HH_{s-1}(x,W)$ and $W\ge1$ (it is
$1$ when $y=1$ and at least $x$ otherwise). The induction is on the rank.

\emph{Rank $4$.} Here $T=x^{W}$, and we show that for every $z\ge1$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nest4}
  W\bigl(\HH_4(T,z)+1\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_4(x,y+z);
\end{equation}
since $W\ge1$ this gives $\HH_4(T,z)<\HH_4(x,y+z)$, which is the assertion. For $z=1$
the two sides are $W(T+1)$ and $x^{T}$. As $T=x^{W}\ge W+1$ we have
$W(T+1)\le(T-1)(T+1)<T^{2}=x^{2W}$, and $2W\le2^{W}\le x^{W}=T$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:mul-le-pow}, so $x^{2W}\le x^{T}$. Suppose \eqref{eq:nest4} holds at $z$,
and put $h=\HH_4(T,z)$ and $M=\HH_4(x,y+z)$, so that $h\ge1$ and $M\ge Wh+W$. Then
$\HH_4(T,z+1)=T^{h}=x^{Wh}$ and $\HH_4(x,y+z+1)=x^{M}$, and since
$x^{Wh}\ge x^{W}\ge W+1$,
\[
  x^{M}\ \ge\ x^{Wh}\,x^{W}\ \ge\ x^{Wh}(W+1)\ \ge\ Wx^{Wh}+W
  \ =\ W\bigl(\HH_4(T,z+1)+1\bigr),
\]
which is \eqref{eq:nest4} at $z+1$.

\emph{From rank $r\ge4$ to rank $r+1$.} Assume the lemma at rank $r$; only its
non-strict form will be used. At rank $r+1$, where $T=\HH_r(x,W)$, we show that for
every $z\ge1$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nestr}
  W+\HH_{r+1}(T,z)\ <\ \HH_{r+1}(x,y+z),
\end{equation}
which again gives the assertion because $W\ge1$. For $z=1$ the two sides are $W+T$ and
$\HH_r(x,T)$, and $W+T<2T\le xT\le\HH_r(x,T)$ because $T=\HH_r(x,W)\ge W+1$. Suppose
\eqref{eq:nestr} holds at $z$, and put $h=\HH_{r+1}(T,z)$ and $M=\HH_{r+1}(x,y+z)$, so
that $h\ge2$ and $W+h\le M-1$. By the lemma at rank $r$ and monotonicity,
\[
  \HH_{r+1}(T,z+1)=\HH_r\bigl(\HH_r(x,W),h\bigr)\ \le\ \HH_r(x,W+h)
  \ \le\ \HH_r(x,M-1)=:V,
\]
and $V\ge M-1\ge W+h>W$. Since $r-1\ge2$ and $V\ge1$,
\[
  W+\HH_{r+1}(T,z+1)\ \le\ W+V\ <\ 2V\ \le\ xV\ \le\ \HH_{r-1}(x,V),
\]
and $\HH_{r-1}(x,V)=\HH_r(x,M)=\HH_{r+1}(x,y+z+1)$; this is \eqref{eq:nestr} at $z+1$.
\end{proof}
